Our story

It's the lathe
and the hands.

Rafael Osuna was born in 1947 into a very humble family in Madrid. By thirteen he was already cutting glass. This is his story, told almost in his own words, as he told it to his grandson.

Madrid

His mother's accounts

He was the first boy of seven children, and he had to grow up early. At eight he was selling churros on the streets of Madrid, bringing home 12.50 pesetas a day. All of it went to his mother.

She couldn't read or write, so he kept the accounts. Back then people bought on credit. When his father brought home his wages at the weekend, they sat down and did the sums: if they owed 150 pesetas at the shops and the wage was 150, they couldn't pay it all, because they had to keep at least 50 to last until the next week.

And he was the one who went to the shop to explain what he had brought and what he hadn't. The shopkeeper always told him the same thing:

"Tell your mother not to worry, whatever she needs she can ask us for, because your mother is one of the ones who pays."

"And that's exactly how it was," he remembers.

The trade

Learning by watching

In those days trades were passed on. A man from Villacañas working in Madrid came back one day saying they needed boys where he worked. Rafael went and they took him on. It was a glass workshop on Calle Fernando de los Ríos, near Quevedo.

Nobody sat him down to teach him. He learned by watching the craftsmen who knew more than he did. He started out handing clay and pumice for polishing. Then he became a polisher. After that he started cutting, until he became a glass cutter.

He was lucky, he says, to work alongside great cutters. And he had a habit the other boys didn't:

"Kids like me just wanted to earn money. They all did the easy jobs at piece rate and left the hard ones nobody wanted. I liked doing the hard ones, because I wanted to be a good cutter."

The days were long: 8:30 to 2, an hour for lunch, 3 to 6:30, and then two hours of overtime until 8:30. And after that, judo.

1964

The university of life

Judo came to him through his boss at the workshop, who knew he liked it. One day he put Rafael face to face with another lad his own age and told them to grab hold of each other and see who could throw whom, but without pushing. The other boy tried to sweep Rafael's supporting foot. Rafael moved his foot out of the way, swept the other boy instead and threw him to the floor, almost without realising.

The boss called him into the office: "I've been fooling you. I do judo, I'm a blue belt. Want to join?" It cost 100 pesetas a month and 25 to hire the kimono, because you couldn't buy them then. He got the 100 together by saving, unspent, the 25 pesetas his mother gave him each weekend. The boss paid the 25 for the kimono.

In those classes he met people with a lot of education, and he understood that this was something he couldn't give up. He wanted to get out of the working-class neighbourhood where he grew up.

"Lots of people have asked me what I studied, and I tell them I went to the best university in the world: the university of life, the one that teaches you best."

Judo and his girlfriend arrived together: on the 1st and 2nd of April 1964. That girlfriend would later become his wife.

Villacañas, 1980

A gym, a debt and a lathe

In 1980 he moved to Villacañas and opened a judo gym. They had no money and everything was done on loans. He was assured he would be backed. When the debt reached five million, he was given four.

It was the hardest moment. How did he get out of it? "By working." Around 1984 or 1985 he brought in a cutting lathe and started cutting glass inside the gym itself. What came off that lathe paid off the debt.

The whole family worked there. Some cut, some cleaned, some polished.

1991 · 1992

Second among the best

While still working in the gym workshop, he entered a glass-cutting competition run by an Italian glass factory, with entrants from Italy, all over Spain and Portugal. In 1991 the result came: second place.

The prize was his choice: tickets to the finals at the Barcelona Olympics, or a week in Italy visiting the factory. He chose Italy, and travelled in 1992. At the factory they had a display case with Roman-era cups.

The piece he entered is the one he holds dearest. A cut made of many crossing lines that, once finished, form different stars. Very few people can do it.

"Nobody has copied it by hand. I've made pieces like that for colleagues who asked me, and then they boasted they'd made them themselves. It didn't bother me. They know they didn't."
1989 · 1990

The glass workshop

His children were neither working nor studying, and everyone had to pitch in to live off the business. So around 1989 or 1990, after the gym, they took the step: they rented a unit on Avenida de Madrid and set up the glass workshop. At one point they had eight workers. Later they built their own unit on the industrial estate, and worked very well there for years. That's where they were when his grandson was born.

Customers didn't come through the internet. They came through sales reps, who went round factories and shops selling the product.

What has changed in the trade since then? Less than you'd think:

"The tools are more or less the same: a lathe and a wheel. There are no miracles. Being a craft, the computer doesn't come into it. It's the lathe and the hands."
Today

We're still here

Then came the crisis. Very big, important factories closed. They didn't.

"We're still going thanks to my daughter. And my grandson helps whenever he can. We're still working."

Today the business is called Áncora and his daughter runs it. And when Rafael is asked what he's proudest of in everything he built, he doesn't talk about glass: "The family we had. And above all, my wife."
